## **1. Basis**

⑴ Definition: A linearly independent spanning set

⑵ **Theorem 1.** The number of elements in a basis of a vector space is unique: This number is called the dimension.

> ① **Theorem 1-1.** Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space and let $S=\lbrace \mathbf{y}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{y}_m \rbrace $ be a subset of $V$. If $n<m$, then $S$ is linearly dependent.

>> ○ **Proof**

>> Let $\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,\cdots,\mathbf{x}_n$ be a basis of $V$.

<br>

<img width="504" height="264" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-24 오후 5 29 43" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ccd1dbcf-40ac-4e74-8f54-28c3da8711e9" />

<br>

>> Since there are $n$ linear equations in $m$ unknowns, the resulting homogeneous system has nontrivial solutions, implying linear dependence.

> ② **Theorem 1-2.** Similarly to Theorem 1, if $n>m$, then $S$ is not a spanning set: That is, the number of vectors is insufficient.

⑶ **Theorem 2.** Existence of a basis of a vector space: If $\dim V=n$ and $A=\lbrace \mathbf{X}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{X}_n \rbrace $ is linearly independent, then $A$ is a basis of $V$.

> ○ **Proof**

> Let $\mathbf{x}$ be an arbitrary basis vector of $V$.

<br>

<img width="445" height="301" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-24 오후 5 30 06" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/0fb72130-630b-4dcf-9cb1-e2fcc2918cd4" />

<br>

> Since $\lbrace \mathbf{X}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{X}_n \rbrace $ is both a spanning set and linearly independent, it is a basis of $V$.

⑷ **Theorem 3.** If $W$ is a subspace of a vector space $V$ and $\dim W=\dim V$, then $W=V$.

> ○ **Proof**

> Let $\lbrace \mathbf{w}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{w}_n \rbrace $ be a basis of $W$ and $\lbrace \mathbf{v}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{v}_n \rbrace $ be a basis of $V$. Suppose there exists $\mathbf{v}$ such that $\mathbf{v}\in V$ and $\mathbf{v}\notin W$. (#)

## **2. Orthogonal Set**

⑴ Orthogonality

<br>

<img width="95" height="33" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-24 오후 5 36 06" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1f0a77e6-59c0-4b30-8851-ece73163c57a" />

<br>

⑵ Orthogonal set: A set in which any two distinct elements are orthogonal. An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is linearly independent.

<br>

<img width="330" height="143" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-24 오후 5 36 32" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/eccf7855-a897-47f3-8528-0a4ad4fc976b" />

<br>

⑶ Orthogonal basis: An orthogonal set $S$ that is a basis of $V$

⑷ Orthonormal basis: An orthogonal basis $S$ satisfying $\left\|\mathbf{x}_j\right\|=1$ for every $j$

> ① For a matrix $U$ whose columns form an orthonormal basis, $U^{\mathsf{T}}U=I\iff U^{-1}=U^{\mathsf{T}}$.

⑸ Standard basis: Refers to $U=\mathcal{E}_n\equiv\lbrace \mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2,\cdots,\mathbf{e}_n \rbrace $, which is one of the bases of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

⑹ Coordinate system

<br>

⑺ Orthogonal coordinate system: A coordinate system determined by the standard basis

⑻ Projection

> ① A transformation that projects an arbitrary vector onto a geometric object (e.g., a line or plane) spanned by an orthonormal basis

⑸ **Theorem 4.** For a positive integer $n$, if an $n\times n$ real matrix $A$ satisfies $A^{n+1}=O$, then $A^n=O$, where $O$ is the zero matrix. ([ref](https://jb243.github.io/pages/653))

> ○ **Proof**

>> We use proof by contradiction. Suppose an $n\times n$ real matrix $A$ satisfies $A^{n+1}=O$ but $A^n\neq O$. Then we can find $\mathbf{v}_1\in\operatorname{Im}(A^n)$ such that $\mathbf{v}_1\neq\mathbf{0}$. (Here, $\mathbf{0}$ denotes the zero vector.) Since $A\mathbf{v}_1\in\operatorname{Im}(A^{n+1})=\operatorname{Im}(O)=\lbrace \mathbf{0} \rbrace $, we have $A\mathbf{v}_1=\mathbf{0}$. Therefore, from $A^n\mathbf{v}_1=\mathbf{0}$, we can easily see that $\mathbf{v}_1\in\ker(A^n)$. Hence, $A^n\mathbf{v}_1\neq\mathbf{0}$ (because $\mathbf{v}_1\in\operatorname{Im}(A^n)$ and $A^n\neq O$), while $A^n\mathbf{v}_1=\mathbf{0}$ (because $\mathbf{v}_1\in\ker(A^n)$), which is a contradiction. Since the assumption is false, if an $n\times n$ real matrix $A$ satisfies $A^{n+1}=O$, then $A^n=O$.
