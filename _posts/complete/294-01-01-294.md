## **2. Prime Number Theorem**

⑴ Statement: A theorem concerning the density of prime numbers.

<br>

<img width="602" height="222" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-10 오전 3 06 28" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7f3f4aad-41af-47f2-a3ef-a72f9b0cf757" />

<br>

> ① For example, when $x=10^6$, the actual number of primes is 78,498, whereas $x/\log x$ is approximately 72,382.

> ② From the asymptotic equivalence above, one might expect that a similar density could arise from other rules defining subsets of natural numbers, not necessarily just prime numbers.

⑵ **Proof 1.** Hadamard (1896): Proved the existence of a zero-free region for the zeta function.

⑶ **Proof 2.** de la Vallée Poussin (1896): Controlled the locations of zeros and estimated the distribution of prime numbers.

⑷ **Proof 3.** Erdős (1948–1949): Used the fact that sufficiently many prime numbers exist even in short intervals.

⑸ **Proof 4.** Selberg (1948–1949): Derived a symmetric formula involving prime numbers and pairs of primes.

⑹ **Proof 5.** Newman (1980): Derived the asymptotic distribution of prime numbers simply from analytic continuation.

⑺ **Proof 7.** Modern analytic proof

> ① **Step 1.** Zeta function inequality: For $\sigma>1$.

>> ○ Since we have already established $\displaystyle \lim_{s\to 1}(s-1)\zeta(s)=1$, the point $s=1$ is a pole rather than a zero: This also gives $\zeta(1+\varepsilon)\sim 1/\varepsilon$.

>> ○ Since $\zeta(1+\varepsilon)\sim 1/\varepsilon$, while $\zeta(1+\varepsilon+it_0)$ is assumed to approach a zero, a certain tension arises, requiring $m\geq 1$.

>> ○ Although $\zeta(1+\varepsilon+it_0)$ is associated with a zero, it is unlikely that $\zeta(1+\varepsilon+2it_0)$ has a related zero or pole, so it is treated as $O(1)$.

>> ○ Proof by contradiction: Assuming that a zero exists leads to a contradiction with the inequality obtained in **Step 1** → Therefore, no such zero exists.

>> ○ One may also interpret this as follows: Since infinitely many different trigonometric identities can be chosen in **Step 1**, they impose so many constraints that the existence of a zero becomes impossible.

## **3. Goldbach's Conjecture**

⑴ Strong Goldbach's Conjecture

> ① The claim that every even integer greater than 2 can be expressed as the sum of two prime numbers. It has not yet been fully proven.

⑵ Weak Goldbach's Conjecture (<span style="color: #ffffff; background-color: #ee2323; font-family: Times New Roman;"><b>►</b></span> [Video Explanation](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/pci6QWe3nCo))

> ① Overview

>> ○ Statement: Every odd integer greater than 5 can be expressed as the sum of three prime numbers.

>> ○ Proved by Harald Andrés Helfgott in 2013. ([ref](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.7748))

>> ○ **Example 1.** $7=2+2+3$

>> ○ **Example 2.** $9=3+3+3$

> ② **Step 1.** Analytic Proof for Sufficiently Large $N$

>> ○ There exists some $N_0$ such that the statement can be proved analytically for all $N \geq N_0$, using analytic methods to estimate the distribution of prime numbers.

>> ○ Specifically, every odd integer $N\geq 10^{27}$ can be expressed as the sum of three prime numbers.

> ③ **1-1.** $R(N)=$ The number of ordered representations of $N$ as the sum of three prime numbers.

>> ○ The statement to be proved is $R(N)>0$.

> ④ **1-2.** Express the distribution of prime numbers using a [Fourier series](https://jb243.github.io/pages/1826).

<br>

<img width="653" height="263" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-10 오전 3 09 37" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/038f136d-a91b-4174-afaf-15ce172d2d65" />

<br>

>> ○ This identity is an exact mathematical equality, not an estimate or approximation.

>> ○ **Major arcs** $\mathfrak{M}$: Subintervals of the integration domain $[0,1]$ for $\alpha$ that lie near rational numbers with small denominators. The distribution of prime numbers can be calculated relatively precisely in these regions.

>> ○ **Minor arcs** $\mathfrak{m}$: The remaining intervals. Although accurately calculating individual values is difficult, the overall magnitude can be bounded by exploiting cancellation due to oscillation.

> ⑤ **1-3.** Lower-bound estimate for the major arcs

>> ○ Intuitive understanding

>>> ○ Number of solutions to $a+b+c=N$, where $a,b,c\geq 1$: $\displaystyle \binom{N-1}{2}=\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{2}\approx\frac{N^2}{2}$

>>> ○ According to the **Prime Number Theorem**, $\pi(N)\sim N/\log N$. Therefore, for large numbers of order $N$, the probability that an arbitrary integer is prime is approximately $P(\text{prime})\approx 1/\log N$.

>>> ○ $P(a,b,c\text{ are all prime})\approx\frac{1}{(\log N)^3}$

>>> ○ **$\therefore$** $\displaystyle R(N)\approx\frac{N^2}{2(\log N)^3}$ (not rigorous)

>>> ○ Since the primality of three integers is not completely independent, we must multiply by a singular series $\mathfrak{S}(N)$: The singular series $\mathfrak{S}(N)$ below can be intuitively derived from a combinatorial perspective by calculating the ratio of two probabilities for each prime $p$ and then multiplying these ratios over all primes.

<br>

<img width="348" height="60" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-10 오전 3 10 08" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/41099bfc-a29b-4464-bea8-5a0100960ca2" />

<br>

>>> ○ **$\therefore$** $\displaystyle R(N)\sim\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{N^2}{2(\log N)^3}$ (not rigorous)

>>> ○ However, this formula is an asymptotic expression for sufficiently large odd integers $N$, and its rigorous justification is provided by the circle method.

<br>

<img width="318" height="75" alt="스크린샷 2026-10-10 오전 3 10 31" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d04b4992-9c59-4d15-b9e1-0fb90634af1a" />

<br>

>> ○ For $\alpha\approx a/q$, the integers $a$ and $q$ are coprime, and $q$ is a positive integer (with $q$ relatively small).

>>> ○ **Prime Number Theorem for Arithmetic Progressions**: $\displaystyle \pi(N;q,a)\sim\frac{N}{\varphi(q)\log N}$

>>> ○ In other words, for $q=3,5,7,101$, the numbers of admissible residue classes $\varphi(q)$ are $2,4,6,100$, respectively, and the proportion of primes distributed among each residue class is the reciprocal of that number.

>>> ○ When $q$ becomes large, two problems arise.

>>> ○ **Problem 1.** Since the number of primes in each group decreases, stronger results are required to estimate the distribution of each group with the same relative error.

>>> ○ **Problem 2.** The complex terms in the exponential sum become distributed in more diverse directions and may cancel one another.

>>> ○ By exploiting the regular phase structure associated with small denominators $q$, one can analytically establish an upper bound on the absolute value of the error.

>> ○ **Conclusion:** $\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{major}}(N)\geq\text{positive main term}-\sup\left\|E(N)\right\|$

>> ○ Parseval's identity in Fourier analysis

## **4. Riemann Hypothesis**

⑴ Riemann Hypothesis: Currently unproven.

> ① Overview

>> ○ Definition: The real part of every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function $\operatorname{Re}(s)=1$ is exactly $1/2$.

>> ○ In the Prime Number Theorem, zeros on the line $\operatorname{Re}(s)=1$ were ruled out. We now aim to extend this zero-exclusion result to a fixed boundary strictly less than 1.

> ② A error condition equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis

>> ○ To establish a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function by analytically continuing its reciprocal into the candidate zero region and proving that the continuation is pole-free.

> ④ [Verification by David Platt](https://arxiv.org/html/1305.3087)

>> ○ **Step 1.** Choose a conductor $q$: $q=4,5,7,8,9,\cdots$

>> ○ **Step 2.** Select all primitive characters $\chi$ with that conductor: Construct $L(s,\chi)$ for each character.

>> ○ **Step 3.** Verify the nontrivial zeros $\rho$ of $L(s,\chi)$: Up to a prescribed height $\left\|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\right\|\leq H_q$.

⑵ Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis

> ① **Overview**

>> ○ **Definition:** The Riemann zeta function has no zeros to the right of some fixed boundary strictly less than 1.

>> ○ OpenAI presents two results, [Re(𝑠)>11/12](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Quasi-Riemann-Hypothesis-October-5-2026/README.md) and [Re(𝑠)>7/8](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-Quasi-Riemann-Hypothesis-September-30-2026/paper.pdf). However, understanding the former is sufficient to understand a proof of the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis.

>> ○ Like Goldbach's conjecture, this is a problem in analytic number theory. However, it had remained unsolved due to the substantially greater complexity and scale of the calculations involved.

> ② **Step 1.** Reformulation in terms of the reciprocal of an $L$-function

>> ○ $L(s,\chi)=0 \iff 1/L(s,\chi)$ has a pole.

> ③ **Step 2.** Expressing a special number-theoretic sum $J_\eta(Z)$ in two ways

>> ○ **Method 1.** Direct Estimation: Estimate how small the entire sum is using the theta-function transformation formula, the large sieve inequality, and related techniques.

>> ○ **Method 2.** Poisson summation formula: $J_\eta(Z)=f_\eta(Z)+E_\eta(Z)$.

>> ○ $f_\eta(Z)$: A Mellin integral associated with $1/L(s,\eta)$.

>> ○ $E_\eta(Z)$: The error term.

> ④ **Step 3.** Applying the triangle inequality

>> ○ $\left\|f_\eta(Z)\right\|\leq \left\|J_\eta(Z)\right\|+\left\|J_\eta(Z)-f_\eta(Z)\right\|$.

>> ○ **Upper Bound 1:** The magnitude of $J_\eta(Z)$ itself is sufficiently small.

>> ○ **Upper Bound 2:** The difference between $J_\eta(Z)$ and $f_\eta(Z)$ is also sufficiently small.

> ⑤ **Step 4.** Analytic continuation via the Mellin transform

>> ○ Applying the Mellin transform allows $1/L(s,\eta)$, which is connected to $f_\eta(Z)$, to be analytically continued to a larger region than originally available.

>> ○ Since $1/L(s,\eta)$ has no poles in this region, it follows that $(L(s,\eta)\neq 0$.

> ⑥ **Step 5.** Improving the zero-free boundary

>> ○ **Part I:** Theta-function transformation formula, Poisson summation formula, and large sieve inequality → No zeros in $\operatorname{Re}(s)>11/12$.

>> ○ **Part II:** Prime-factor corrections, asymmetric scaling, and additional moment estimates → No zeros in $\operatorname{Re}(s)>7/8$.
