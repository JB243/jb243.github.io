## 2. Example 1. Pappus Chain

⑴ Question

<br>

<img width="860" height="227" alt="스크린샷 2026-08-16 오후 8 05 33" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d9f1a37c-2493-4420-8f9d-878a92156c43" />

**Figure 1.** Pappus Chain

<br>

Starting with the circle $P_1$ tangent to the three semicircles that form an arbelos, construct a sequence of circles $P_i$, each tangent to one of the two smaller inner circles and the large outer circle. This chain of circles is called the Pappus chain. In the Pappus chain, the distance between the center of the first inscribed circle $P_1$ and the baseline is twice the radius of the circle, and the distance between the center of the second circle $P_2$ and the baseline is four times its radius. In general, the distance between the center of the $n$th circle $P_n$ and the baseline is $2n$ times its radius. Furthermore, the centers of the circles $P_i$ lie on a single ellipse.

<br>

⑵ Solution

---

First, let us introduce the following notation.

<br>

<img width="1045" height="413" alt="스크린샷 2026-08-16 오후 8 08 44" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/70c45444-178a-4bf0-8449-79de0f6cdf77" />

**Figure 2.** Proof of Pappus Chain

<br>

| **Object**                 | **Center** | **Radius** | **Height of Center** |
| -------------------------- | ---------- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| Large outer semicircle     | $C$        | $R$        | $0$                  |
| Left inner semicircle      | $L$        | $r_L$      | $0$                  |
| Right inner semicircle     | $M$        | $r_M$      | $0$                  |
| $n$-th circle in the chain | $P_n$      | $\rho_n$   | $h_n$                |

<br>

Since $P_n$ is internally tangent to the large outer semicircle and externally tangent to the left inner semicircle, we have $CP_n = R-\rho_n$ and $LP_n = r_L+\rho_n$. Therefore,

$$
CP_n+LP_n=(R-\rho_n)+(r_L+\rho_n)=R+r_L.
$$

Thus, the sum of the distances from $P_n$ to the fixed points $C$ and $L$ is constant. Therefore, the centers of the circles forming the Pappus chain lie on an ellipse with $C$ and $L$ as its two foci. 

To show that the height of the center of $P_n$ is $2n\rho_n$, let us use inversive geometry with respect to point $A$. The inversion of a point $P$ with respect to a reference circle centered at $A$ with radius $k$ is the point $P'$, satisfying $AP\cdot AP'=k^2$, with points $A$, $P$, and $P'$ lying on a single straight line. Letting $AP=2R\cos\theta$, we obtain $AP'=\dfrac{k^2}{2R\cos\theta}$. Therefore, the inverse image of a circle passing through $A$ is a line perpendicular to the $x$-axis and passing through $x=\dfrac{k^2}{2R}$. Therefore, in the figure above, we can identify two parallel lines passing through $x=\dfrac{k^2}{2R}$ and $x=\dfrac{k^2}{2r_L}$. 

By the principle of continuity, the inverse image of the inner circle on the right is still a circle. Therefore, the inverse images of the $1$st, $2$nd, $\cdots$, $n$th, $\cdots$ chain circles are also circles, and by the principle of continuity, they are transformed into congruent circles of radius $\sigma$ tangent between the two parallel lines. Since the congruent circles are stacked vertically at intervals equal to their diameter $2\sigma$, the height of the inverse image of the $n$th chain circle is $H_n=2n\sigma$. If the position of the center and the radius of the inverted circle are scaled by a factor of $\lambda_n$, then by similarity, the height of the inverted circle is also scaled by the same factor $\lambda_n$. Therefore,

$$
2n=\frac{2n\sigma}{\sigma}=\frac{H_n}{\sigma}=\frac{\lambda_n h_n}{\lambda_n\rho_n}=\frac{h_n}{\rho_n}.
$$

Hence,

$$
h_n=2n\rho_n.
$$

Meanwhile, defining $r=\dfrac{r_L}{R}$, the original proof shows by mathematical induction that $P_n=(x_n,y_n)$ and $\rho_n=r_n$.

## 3. Example 2. Peaucellier-Lipkin linkage

⑴ Problem: Prove that the trajectory of $C$ is a straight line in the following figure. (<span style="color: #ffffff; background-color: #ee2323; font-family: Times New Roman;"><b>►</b></span> [Video explanation](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/tF9Zzl-3Fgg))

<br>

<img width="520" height="382" alt="스크린샷 2026-11-08 오후 1 17 52" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e7fafd11-5718-48b7-9bef-aab2de3a90b8" />

**Figure 3.** Peaucellier-Lipkin linkage and inversive geometry

<br>

⑵ Solution

---

Let $M$ be the intersection of the diagonals of rhombus $ADBC$, and let the lengths of the longer and shorter links be $a$ and $b$, respectively. By the Pythagorean theorem,

$$OA^2=OM^2+AM^2=a^2,\quad AD^2=DM^2+AM^2=b^2$$

Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} OD\cdot OC &=(OM-DM)(OM+DM)\\\ &=OM^2-DM^2\\\ &=\boxed{a^2-b^2=R^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Since $O,D,C$ are collinear, $C$ and $D$ are inverse points with respect to the circle of inversion centered at $O$ with radius $R$. Since $E$ is a fixed point and $ED=EO$, point $D$ moves along a circle centered at $E$ with radius $EO$. This circle passes through the center of inversion $O$. In inversion geometry, the inverse of a circle passing through the center of inversion is a straight line. Therefore, the inverse point $C$ of $D$ traces a straight line. $\square$

---

⑶ Significance: It converts rotational movement into linear movement.
