## **1. Overview**

⑴ Theorem: If the sum of reciprocals of a set $A$ of natural numbers diverges to infinity, then the set necessarily contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrarily large finite length.

> ① **Example 1.** Since the set of all natural numbers satisfies $1 + \frac12 + \frac13 + \cdots = \infty$, it contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrary length, such as 10, 100, and 1000.

> ② **Example 2.** Since the set of perfect squares satisfies $1 + \frac14 + \frac19 + \cdots < \infty$, it does not satisfy the hypothesis of this conjecture.

>> ○ In fact, Fermat proved that there is no arithmetic progression consisting of four distinct perfect squares ([ref](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1572097/)).

> ③ The statement asserts the existence of arithmetic progressions of every finite length, not the existence of a single infinite arithmetic progression.

> ④ The proof below was [proposed by OpenAI on September 23, 2026](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Quasipolynomial-Bounds-for-Arithmetic-Progressions-September-23-2026/paper.pdf), but **has not yet been verified**.

⑵ Incidentally, Paul Erdős is the central figure in the book *The Man Who Loved Only Numbers: The Story of Paul Erdős and the Search for Mathematical Truth*.

## **2. Proof**

⑴ **Step 1.** Assume that there are too few arithmetic progressions → The set is not distributed randomly.

⑵ **Step 2.** Higher-order Fourier analysis: Detect hidden regularity.

> ① Arithmetic progression of length 3: $n, n+d, n+2d$

>> ○ **Classical Fourier analysis**: This periodic structure can be represented by the complex number $\exp(2\pi i\theta n)$, which represents a rotation through an angle of $2\pi\theta n$.

> ② Arithmetic progression of length 4: $n, n+d, n+2d, n+3d$

>> ○ For the quadratic function $q(n)=\theta n^2$, the identity $q(n+3d)-3q(n+2d)+3q(n+d)-q(n)=0$ always holds.

>> ○ Such quadratic regularity is difficult to detect using classical Fourier analysis.

>>> ○ Reason: Classical Fourier analysis deals with constant rotation rates, whereas $q(n)$ does not have a constant rotation rate, as shown by $q(n+d)-q(n)=\theta(2nd+d^2)$.

>> ○ Classical Fourier analysis and this quadratic regularity can be described using the same language of finite differences, with only the polynomial degree being different.

>>> ○ Finite differences of a linear function: $1\xrightarrow{+2}3\xrightarrow{+2}5\xrightarrow{+2}7$

>>> ○ Finite differences of a quadratic function: $0\xrightarrow{+1}1\xrightarrow{+3}4\xrightarrow{+5}9\xrightarrow{+7}16$

>>> ○ The finite-difference structure of a quadratic function also explains why identities such as $(n+h+k)^2-(n+h)^2-(n+k)^2+n^2=2hk$ hold.

>>> ○ Therefore, the third finite difference of a quadratic function is identically zero: $\Delta_\ell\Delta_k\Delta_h(\theta n^2)=0$.

>>> ○ Because the third finite difference of $q(n)=\theta n^2$ is zero, the Gowers $U^3$ norm of the complex-valued function $f(n)=\exp(2\pi iq(n))$ is exactly 1.

>> ○ Inverse theorem for Gowers norms

>>> ○ Theorem: A quadratic phase produces a large $U^3$ norm, and conversely, the inverse theorem concerns whether a large $U^3$ norm necessarily implies the presence of quadratic structure.

>>> ○ On a suitable finite cyclic group, correlations associated with arithmetic progressions of length 4 are controlled by the $U^3$ norm: This follows from repeated applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

> ④ Nilsequence

>> ○ $\exp(\alpha n\lfloor\beta n\rfloor)$ (where $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the floor function) exhibits quadratic variation similar to $n^2$, but also displays discontinuous changes because of the floor function → Such expressions are called generalized quadratic phases (bracket quadratic phases) → To establish the inverse theorem for Gowers norms in general, a broader class of functions than quadratic polynomial phases is required.

>> ○ Nilsequence: A sequence obtained by observing regular motion on a space generated by a nilpotent group.

>> ○ It captures not only linear, quadratic, and higher-degree polynomial phases, but also more complicated forms of regularity that cannot be expressed as ordinary polynomial phases.

>> ○ The inverse theorem for Gowers norms guarantees that a function with a large $U^k$ norm has a significant correlation with a suitable $(k-1)$-step nilsequence.

⑶ **Step 3.** Higher-order Fourier analysis → Density increment

> ① Suppose that a set $A$ has density $\alpha$ in a finite space $X$: $f=\mathbf{1}$<sub>𝛢</sub>, $\mathbb{E}_{n\in X}f(n)=\alpha$.

>> ○ Density: The proportion of elements belonging to $A$ among all natural numbers under consideration.

>> ○ The set $A$ is an arbitrary set assumed to contain no arithmetic progression of length $k$, and the divergence condition on the reciprocal sum is used in the final step.

> ② Suppose that higher-order Fourier analysis shows that $f-\alpha$ has a positive correlation with some real-valued nilsequence $F(n)$ satisfying $\left\|F(n)\right\|\leq 1$.

>> ○ $\mathbb{E}_{n\in X}[(f(n)-\alpha)F(n)]\geq\delta>0$

>> ○ This means that the elements of $A$ are distributed nonuniformly according to how the values of $F$ vary.

> ③ If $F$ is a function on a nilmanifold $G/\Gamma$, then the space can be partitioned into sufficiently small regions $(U_1,\ldots,U_m)$.

>> ○ By making the partition sufficiently fine, $F(n)$ becomes approximately constant on each $C_j=\{n:g(n)\Gamma\in U_j\}$.

> ④ **General principle of density increments**: Since the unweighted sum is zero while the weighted sum is positive, a combinatorial argument guarantees the existence of a region where the density increases in accordance with variations in $F$.

> ⑤ Conclusion

>> ○ However, this principle is not specific to higher-order Fourier analysis; it is a general principle that follows from correlations.

>> ○ Ultimately, the question is whether one can find a region exhibiting a substantial density increment. To apply higher-order Fourier analysis again, one needs a method for adding new regularity while preserving the regularity already discovered.

⑷ **Step 4.** Density increment → Triangular polynomial cell → Next density increment

> ① **4-1.** Triangular polynomial cell: A structure that records polynomial conditions hierarchically.

>> ○ **Example 1.** A region where the quadratic phase $\exp(2\pi i\theta n^2)$ is nearly constant.

>>> ○ $C=\{n:\left\|\theta n^2-t\right\|\leq w\}$

>>> ○ This can be represented as a region where there exists a suitable integer $b_2$ satisfying $\left\|b_2-(\theta n^2-t)\right\|\leq w$.

>> ○ **Example 2.** For more general nilsequences, lower-degree integer coordinates influence higher-degree polynomials, leading to conditions of the following form.

>>> ○ Suppose that the following condition is required for a density increment: $\left\|\theta n-b_1\right\|\leq w_1$, $b_1\in\mathbb{Z}$.

>>> ○ For the next density increment, suppose that the following condition must be satisfied: $\left\|\beta n^2+\lambda nb_1-b_2\right\|\leq w_2$, $b_2\in\mathbb{Z}$, where $b_1$ is the variable introduced by the first condition.

>>> ○ Repeatedly substituting approximate values in this way destroys the previously established regularity: In particular, as the term $nb_1$ illustrates, the approximation error can become very large when $n$ is large.

>>> ○ By preserving $b_1$ exactly rather than replacing it with its approximation $\theta n$, errors can be controlled throughout the iterative process.

> ② **4-2.** Representation of a density increment: For a weight function $B(n)$,

>> ○ $\displaystyle \rho_B=\frac{\sum_n\mathbf{1}_A(n)B(n)}{\sum_n B(n)}>(1+\eta)a$

>> ○ In other words, assigning weights to numbers satisfying specific polynomial regularity conditions produces a density greater than the original reference density $a$.

>> ○ The actual paper formalizes this through a density certificate using two weights, $B_-$ and $B_+$, to control errors.

> ③ **4-3.** Next density increment

>> ○ By choosing a suitable affine map $\psi(t)=x+Vt$, part of the complicated region can be examined inside a **new integer box**.

>> ○ Since affine transformations **preserve the structure of arithmetic progressions**, higher-order Fourier analysis can be applied again inside this box to find another density increment.

>> ○ The newly discovered conditions are then translated back into the original variables and added to the existing triangular polynomial cell, allowing the same procedure to be repeated.

> ④ Key technical improvements

>> ○ Previous difficulty: As the iteration proceeds, the conditions become increasingly complicated and the tolerance $w$ becomes smaller, potentially making the region available for analysis excessively small.

>> ○ The paper measures the precision at each level by $Q_h=\log(2/w_h)$ and **controls the additional precision cost** when introducing new conditions.

>> ○ In particular, the key is to ensure that the additional precision cost at a given level does not depend on that level's own current precision.

>> ○ As a result, the total cost can be controlled polynomially in the number of iterations, making it possible to repeat density increments when the initial interval is sufficiently large.

> ⑤ **Conclusion**: Triangular polynomial cells make repeated density increments possible while preserving the regularity discovered through higher-order Fourier analysis.

⑸ **Step 5.** Deriving a contradiction

> ① Starting from an initial density $a$ (e.g., 20%), sufficient space can be maintained for approximately $O(\log(1/\alpha))$ iterations of the form $a\to(1+\eta)a\to(1+\eta)^2a\to\cdots$.

> ② Eventually, this process could produce a region whose density exceeds 1, resulting in a contradiction.

> ③ Therefore, for a set of density $\alpha$ that avoids arithmetic progressions of length $k$ to exist, the size $N$ of the initial interval must satisfy a certain restriction.

⑹ **Step 6.** Proof of the Erdős Reciprocal-Sum Conjecture

> ① **Result of the preceding proof:** An upper bound is obtained on how large a set without arithmetic progressions of length $k$ can be.

>> ○ $r_k(N)$: The maximum number of elements that can be selected from the natural numbers between 1 and $N$ without forming an arithmetic progression of length $k$.

>> ○ By repeatedly applying the density-increment method, the following upper bound is obtained (where $C_k,c_k,\varepsilon_k>0$): $r_k(N)\leq C_kN\exp[-c_k(\log N)^{\varepsilon_k}]$.

>> ○ This means that sets avoiding arithmetic progressions must become increasingly sparse as $N$ grows.

> ② **Connection to reciprocal sums:** Divide the natural numbers into intervals whose sizes double successively: $[1,2),[2,4),[4,8),[8,16),\ldots$.

>> ○ **Condition 1.** A general interval $D_m=[2^m,2^{m+1})$ contains $2^m$ natural numbers.

>> ○ **Condition 2.** If $A$ contains no arithmetic progression of length $k$, the number of elements of $A$ in this interval cannot exceed $r_k(2^m)$.

>> ○ **Condition 3.** Since every element $a$ in the interval satisfies $a\geq 2^m$, its reciprocal satisfies $\frac1a\leq\frac1{2^m}$.

>> ○ The reciprocal sum over all intervals is therefore bounded as follows:

<br>

>> ○ $\displaystyle \sum_{a\in A}\frac1a\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left\|A\cap D_m\right\|}{2^m}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{r_k(2^{m+1})}{2^m}\leq 2C_k\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp[-c_k((m+1)\log 2)^{\varepsilon_k}]$

>> ○ The series on the right converges because $\varepsilon_k>0$ → Thus, the reciprocal sum of any set containing no arithmetic progression of length $k$ must converge.

>> ○ Since $k$ can be chosen arbitrarily, every set whose reciprocal sum diverges must contain arithmetic progressions of every finite length.

> ③ **Difference between the set of all natural numbers and the set of perfect squares**

>> ○ Set of all natural numbers: The interval $[2^m,2^{m+1})$ contains $2^m$ elements, and the reciprocal of each element is approximately $2^{-m}$. Therefore, the reciprocal sum over each interval remains of constant order → Summing over all intervals gives a divergent series.

>> ○ Set of perfect squares: The same interval contains only approximately $2^{m/2}$ perfect squares, so the reciprocal sum over each interval decreases on the order of $2^{-m/2}$ → The sum converges even after adding contributions from all intervals.

>> ○ The difference arises from how many elements remain in each interval as the numbers become larger.

> ④ **Conclusion:** The key point is that divergence of the reciprocal sum is a stronger condition than merely having infinitely many elements.

## **3. Applications**

⑴ Van der Waerden's theorem: If the natural numbers are colored using finitely many colors, there exists a monochromatic arithmetic progression of any prescribed finite length.

⑵ Roth's theorem → Szemerédi's theorem: A set of positive density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

> ① Positive density is a stronger condition than the hypothesis of the reciprocal-sum conjecture.

⑶ Green–Tao theorem: Arithmetic progressions of prime numbers.

> ① Euler had already proved that $\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{p\text{ prime}}}\frac1p=\infty$: This fact can also be easily derived from the [prime number theorem](https://jb243.github.io/pages/294).

> ② The Green–Tao theorem does not rely on the Erdős reciprocal-sum conjecture, but it can be easily deduced if the preceding reciprocal-sum conjecture is available.
